A Disciplined Framework for Agentic AI Red-Teaming

How I built a skills-based system for testing LLM applications, and why its most important components are the two that stop it from producing noise.

Austin Poole AI Security Engineering Methodology overview · no operational payloads

Most write-ups about AI red-teaming are lists of attacks. This one is about the opposite: the judgment layer that decides which candidate findings are real, which are noise, and when the correct answer is “this target is clean, submit nothing.”

I built a structured framework for adversarially testing LLM-integrated applications: product chatbots, agentic platforms, model-provider tooling, and the AI infrastructure underneath them. It is organized as a library of composable skills (one technique domain per file), a router that selects the right specialists for a given target, and the part I care most about, two governance layers that gate everything the framework produces.

This page describes the architecture and that discipline. It deliberately contains no attack payloads, no exploit code, and no target-specific detail. The framework is only ever run against systems I am authorized to test.

01 The problem it solves

Triage teams for AI bug-bounty programs are drowning. Generative tooling makes it trivial to produce confident, official-looking “findings” that fall apart under scrutiny: a jailbreak that only yields disallowed text, a system-prompt “leak” that reveals nothing sensitive, an isolation “bug” provable only against your own account. The scarce skill is not generating attacks. It is judgment: knowing what constitutes reportable impact, and having the discipline to discard everything that does not.

So I designed the framework around a hard constraint: no candidate finding reaches a report without passing an explicit gate, and concluding a target is secure is a successful outcome, not a failure. The technique coverage, the routing, and the taxonomy alignment all exist to serve that constraint.

02 The kill-switch

Before any finding is written up, it runs a seven-question gate. The questions test for real attacker gain, in-scope assets, accepted-impact classes, plausible privilege assumptions, provable evidence, novelty, and exclusion lists. The output is one of four verdicts, and two of them stop the finding cold.

Candidate finding → gate → verdict
candidate finding → 7-question gate → verdict
PASSAll checks clear. Write the report at full severity.
DOWNGRADEReal bug, weaker evidence. Write it honestly, lower severity, no inflation.
CHAIN‑REQUIREDReal primitive, not reportable alone. Hold it, find the second half.
KILLFails a structural check. Drop it, log why, move on.

The verdict I built the whole system to respect is the last one. On a hardened target, most candidates should die here. The professional move is to close the target as clean with an honest non-finding summary, rather than manufacture a bug that is not there. A framework that cannot say “nothing to report” is a framework that floods triage and burns its own credibility.

03 Treating the framework’s own knowledge as an untrusted supply chain

The framework is designed to learn: completed engagements can be summarized into new lessons or new skill files. That capability is also a threat. An LLM asked to summarize or generate technique documentation will produce confident, specific-looking claims (exact win-rates, quoted model output, version numbers) whether or not any of it happened.

So every piece of content entering the knowledge base, whether self-generated or imported from an outside source, passes a verification gate before it is trusted. It checks provenance, resolves every checkable fact (CVEs, product and model names, version strings), demands real evidence behind any “confirmed” claim, and scans imported files for the same injection and unicode-smuggling tricks the framework studies in its targets. The dispositions mirror the kill-switch:

PASS

verified
  • Provenance traces to a real engagement or primary source
  • Facts resolve; evidence exists
  • Merge as-is

DOWNGRADE

real idea, unverifiable specifics
  • Keep the underlying technique
  • Strip fabricated numbers, quotes, version claims
  • Mark “untested, validate per target”

This is the research on agent-skill supply-chain compromise applied to my own pipeline rather than only to targets. It is also the reason I trust the framework’s output: the self-improvement loop cannot quietly promote a plausible fabrication into a “fact.”

A concrete instance: a technique catalog imported from an external CTF context carried specific “confirmed” win-rates and model-version claims that had been self-simulated against a toy challenge. The gate kept the generalizable technique principles and stripped every fabricated benchmark before merge.

04 Architecture

Underneath the two governance layers, the framework is organized so that a target is classified before it is attacked. The routing decision matters more than any single technique.

Layer 1 · Router
Archetype & observation routing
Classifies the target (product-embedded AI, model provider, or AI infrastructure), then maps what it observes (a connector, a RAG pipeline, an MCP server, a reasoning trace) to the right specialist skills. Prevents defaulting to one familiar playbook.
Layer 2 · Specialists
Composable technique domains
One domain per skill: enumeration, indirect injection, data-flow analysis, tool and connector abuse, cross-tenant isolation, output handling, supply chain, and observability exposure. Each documents where impact comes from and how to prove it, and cross-references the others so findings compose into chains.
Layer 3 · Coverage map
Taxonomy alignment
A coverage checklist built on an open prompt-injection taxonomy, so a hunt can be audited for completeness rather than stopping at the three techniques one reaches for by habit. Findings carry taxonomy codes that cross-map to industry standards.
Layer 4 · Governance
Kill-switch & verification gate
The two layers above. Nothing is reported without passing the seven-question gate; nothing enters the knowledge base without passing verification. This is the layer that makes the rest safe to trust.

A parallel, slimmer variant exists for CTF contexts, tuned for speed rather than reportability. The contrast clarified, by opposition, exactly what the bug-bounty gate is for. Optimizing one system for “capture the flag fast” and another for “never submit noise” makes the reportability discipline legible in a way neither does alone.

05 Standards alignment

Findings are framed against the vocabularies triage and audit teams already use, so a report maps cleanly onto an established risk register rather than inventing its own:

OWASP LLM Top 10 MITRE ATLAS NIST AI RMF STRIDE (agentic threat surface) CVSS-informed severity

The same alignment underpins a companion artifact: a full STRIDE threat model and adversarial assessment of a agentic platform, with a reconciliation pass that separated confirmed findings from detector false positives. That document is the applied counterpart to the methodology described here.

06 What I take from it

The through-line across every layer is the same: in security, the discipline to discard is worth more than the ability to generate. Anyone can assemble attack techniques from public research. The parts that took real judgment to build are the ones that throw work away: the gate that kills a weak finding, the verification pass that refuses to trust an unverified claim, the honest “this target is clean” summary. That is the posture I bring to securing agentic AI systems.

Scope & ethics

This framework is run only against systems I am authorized to test: published bug-bounty and vulnerability-disclosure programs, my own infrastructure, and explicit engagements. Its built-in triage gate enforces scope as its second question.
